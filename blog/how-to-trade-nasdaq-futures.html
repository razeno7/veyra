How to Trade Nasdaq Futures: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Distribb · November 2026

How to trade Nasdaq futures comes down to a clear sequence: learn the contract, plan your risk, then follow your rules when the market moves. NQ and MNQ track the Nasdaq-100, but their dollar values differ by a factor of ten. I’ll show you how to prepare and execute a trade, and where live NQ signals may fit into your own process.

Step 1: Learn Nasdaq Futures Contract Specs and Trading Hours

Start with the contract’s point value and minimum tick. These figures tell you how much a price move changes your profit or loss before fees. The full-size contract has a larger point value. The micro contract has a smaller point value.

Both track the Nasdaq-100, an index of large nonfinancial companies. The index can react sharply to interest-rate expectations, inflation reports, and earnings from major companies. A long position gains if the contract price rises, while a short position gains if it falls. Either position can lose quickly.

For example, a 10-point move against one full-size contract is a $200 loss before fees. The same move against one micro contract is $20. The exchange sets contract specifications, but your broker may set different margin requirements. Check the current specs on the CME Group website or a reference page listing contract specifications before trading.

These contracts trade nearly around the clock during the business week on an electronic futures platform, with a scheduled daily maintenance break. The regular US stock-market session opens at 9:30 a.m. ET. Trading around that open can move fast, so know when your own session begins and when you’ll stop.

Choose the active contract month in your platform. Index futures usually have quarterly contracts, and trading activity shifts toward the next contract before expiration. Check the exchange calendar and your broker’s symbol details before entering an order. If a platform shows ND, don’t assume it’s the same product as the full-size contract. Confirm the exact contract and exchange.

Key Takeaway: Learn the tick value and session hours before choosing a contract or placing an order.

Step 2: Choose a Futures Broker and Practice Platform

You’ll need a futures brokerage account and a platform that can send orders to that account. Some brokers include a platform; others let you connect a separate one. Don’t open an account based on a low advertised commission alone. Compare the full cost of trading the contract you intend to use.

Write down the costs that apply to your account: commission per side, exchange and regulatory fees, clearing or routing fees, market data, and any platform subscription. Then calculate a round-trip cost for one contract. A small fee can matter when your planned target is only a few ticks away.

Next, check the broker’s margin rules. Initial and maintenance margin can differ, and intraday requirements may change. Margin is the funds your broker requires to hold a position. It isn’t the maximum you can lose. A small deposit can control a much larger position, so low margin can make losses arrive faster than expected.

Before funding an account, confirm that the platform shows the correct contract month and lets you see your order status, fills, and open position. Test the order ticket in simulation. Practice entering a limit order, setting a stop, and canceling a working order. Make sure you know whether the stop is linked to the entry or must be placed separately.

Simulation helps you learn the platform without risking trading capital, but it can’t fully copy live fills, slippage, or the pressure of a fast market. Use it to rehearse the mechanics. Then review your broker’s current fees, market-data access, and account rules before moving to a live account.

Step 3: Write a Nasdaq Futures Trading Plan

A trading plan should tell you what must happen before you enter, where the trade idea is wrong, and when you’ll stop for the day. Keep it short enough to read before the open. If the plan only says buy when the chart looks strong, it needs more detail.

Choose one setup to study first. A trend-following plan might look for price to hold above a level and form a higher low before a long entry. A breakout plan might require a clear move through a marked range, followed by a retest that holds. A mean-reversion plan might wait for price to move far from a reference level and then show a failure to continue.

These are different ideas, not interchangeable entry signals. Define the conditions you need to see and the price action that cancels the setup. For a breakout, for example, decide whether you need a close beyond the level or a retest. If the move happens without your confirmation, let it go rather than chasing.

Mark levels before the session. You might note the prior day’s high and low, the overnight range, or a level that has mattered more than once. Keep the chart readable. A screen filled with lines can make every price look important.

Then check the day’s scheduled events. Federal Reserve decisions and economic reports can shift expectations for rates and growth. Since many Nasdaq-100 companies are sensitive to interest-rate expectations, a report can cause a sharp move in either direction. Review the Federal Reserve’s meeting calendar, and check official release timing for other events you follow.

Earnings can also affect the index, especially when large constituents report. Decide whether your plan allows trading just before a scheduled release. You don’t need to predict the news. You need to know when it could make your usual stop or entry method less reliable.

Write your entry trigger, stop location, target, maximum risk, and daily loss limit in plain words. Add a rule for when you’ll skip a trade, such as a missed entry or a price that has already moved too far. This makes the plan useful when the open gets noisy.

Step 4: Set Position Size and Risk Limits Before Entry

Choose your maximum dollar loss before you decide how many contracts to trade. Then set the stop distance based on the trade idea. The number of contracts follows from those two choices, not from how confident you feel.

Use this calculation:

Risk per contract = stop distance in points × dollar value per point.
Contracts = your trade risk limit ÷ risk per contract, rounded down.

Say your planned risk is $100 and your stop is 10 points away. One NQ contract risks $200 at that stop, so it’s too large for the limit. One MNQ risks $20, so the calculation allows up to five contracts before fees and slippage. You might still choose fewer. The math gives a ceiling, not a command to use all available risk.

Now account for execution. A stop order may fill beyond its stop price in a fast market. Fees add to the loss, too. So a planned $100 risk isn’t a guarantee that the realized loss will be exactly $100. Leave room for those costs rather than sizing right up to your limit.

Set a daily loss limit as well. When you hit it, stop trading for the day. Don’t raise it after a losing trade or add contracts to recover. If you’re in a prop-firm account, compare your personal limit with the firm’s daily loss and drawdown rules. Follow whichever limit is tighter.

Micros make it easier to adjust exposure in smaller steps. But they don’t remove leverage or risk. Several MNQ contracts can add up to substantial exposure, and commissions may apply to each one. Count total contracts and the total dollar risk, not just the size of each contract.

A position size calculator for NQ and MNQ can help check the arithmetic against your stop and account limit. Verify the result yourself, then confirm the contract’s current point value with your broker or the exchange.

Key Takeaway: If one contract exceeds your planned risk at the stop distance your setup needs, use a smaller contract or skip the trade.

Step 5: Execute and Manage a Trade During the US Open

Before the 9:30 a.m. ET open, check the contract month, your account balance, and the day’s event calendar. Open the chart you’ll use for decisions. Confirm that the price feed is current and that your platform is connected to the right account.

At the open, wait for the condition in your plan. A fast first move isn’t a reason to abandon your entry rule. If your setup needs a retest, wait for it. If price runs without you, let it go. Missing a trade costs nothing; entering without your conditions can put your risk plan in doubt.

When the setup appears, check the order side and quantity. Choose the order type your plan calls for. A market order seeks an immediate fill, but the fill price can differ from the price you saw. A limit order sets a price boundary, but it may not fill. Know what you’re choosing before you submit.

Place the stop as soon as your position is open, unless your platform has already attached it as part of a bracket order. Check that the stop sits at the price that invalidates your trade idea. A stop placed too close just to make the position size work may not fit the setup.

Some platforms support OCO orders, where filling one order cancels another, or trailing stops that follow price by a set amount. Conditional orders can trigger when a chosen price or condition is reached. Learn how each order behaves in simulation first. A trailing stop can exit a trade before your planned target, and a linked order can behave differently across platforms.

Once in the trade, follow the management rule you wrote down. Don’t widen the stop just because price is moving against you. If your plan calls for a target, don’t change it out of fear or excitement. If the market is moving too quickly for you to act calmly, flattening or stepping away may be safer than adding risk.

For a hedge, first estimate how closely NQ matches the exposure you want to offset. A single contract may not match a stock portfolio’s value or mix. Correlations can change, too. Treat a hedge as a separate plan with its own size and exit conditions, not as a way to make a losing trade harmless.

Step 6: Review Trades and Improve Your Process

After the session, record every trade while the details are fresh. Note the contract, direction, entry, stop, exit, size, fees, and result. Add the setup you took and whether you followed your plan. Keep losses in the log, too. They show whether the setup failed or whether you broke a rule.

Track results in dollars and in R. R is the amount you planned to risk on one trade. If your initial risk was $100, a $100 loss is −1R. A $200 gain is +2R. R lets you compare trades taken with different contract sizes without treating a larger position as a better trade by itself.

Review execution separately from outcome. A losing trade can follow the plan; a winning trade can break it. Ask whether the entry matched your trigger, whether the stop stayed in place, and whether the exit followed your rule. Don’t judge a method from one trade. Look for repeated patterns across a useful set of logged trades.

Group your notes by setup and time of day. You may find that your breakout entries work better after a retest than on the first push. Or your records may show that you trade poorly after taking a second loss. These are questions to test against your own data, not conclusions to assume in advance.

Include slippage and fees in the review. If you leave them out, a small-target scalping plan may look better on paper than it does in your account. Note whether an order filled as expected, especially around fast moves or scheduled news.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need to trade Nasdaq futures?

There isn’t one account amount that fits every trader. Your broker sets margin requirements, and those can change. Work out the dollar risk at your stop before choosing a contract. If one standard futures contract risks more than your limit, a micro-sized contract may allow smaller steps, but it still carries risk and can lose money.

Is a standard or micro-sized futures contract better for beginners?

Contract size affects the dollar gain or loss from a price move and the position-sizing choices available. A micro-sized contract can make it easier to size a position in smaller steps. Neither contract is low-risk by default. Practice in simulation and calculate the loss at your stop before trading live.

What time does Nasdaq futures trading start?

Nasdaq futures trade nearly around the clock during the business week, with a daily maintenance break and exchange holiday schedules. The regular US stock-market session opens at 9:30 a.m. ET. Check current hours with your broker and the exchange, especially around holidays or contract changes.

Can I follow futures signals in a prop firm account?

That depends on your firm’s current rules. Check whether third-party signals are allowed, then review its position limits, daily loss limit, and drawdown terms. You’re responsible for your account and order execution. A signal provider can’t make those rules disappear or guarantee that your fills will match a called entry.
