Futures vs CFD: Costs, Risk and Fit

By Distribb · November 2026

Futures and CFDs both track market moves, but they’re built differently: futures trade as set contracts on an exchange, while CFDs are agreements with a broker. For US retail traders, that distinction goes further: futures are available through US markets, while CFDs are generally unavailable through US-regulated firms.

Here’s how the two compare on contract size, costs, risk, and day-to-day use, including what the differences mean for NQ traders.

Market structure: exchange-traded futures vs broker-issued CFDs

A futures contract is a standardized agreement listed on an exchange. Its contract size and trading rules are set for everyone. Trades go through a clearing structure, which stands between buyers and sellers.

A CFD, or contract for difference, is a deal between you and a broker. You don’t own the asset or trade the contract on a central futures exchange. The broker sets the product’s terms and quotes its price, which can differ across providers.

That’s the basic difference behind the phrase contract for difference: the trader and broker settle the change in price between opening and closing the trade. Both instruments can give you market exposure larger than the cash you put up, without owning the underlying asset, but the market structure is different.

For a Nasdaq-100 trader, that affects what you can see. A futures order enters the exchange’s market. A CFD quote comes from the broker, often based on a related market. So the chart may look familiar, but it isn’t the same contract or order book.

Futures specs are often easier to compare because the exchange sets them. CFD terms can vary by broker. Check how the broker sets its price, handles execution, and defines the contract before you trade. Don’t assume two products with a similar index name work alike.

Key Takeaway: Futures are exchange-listed contracts with shared terms; a CFD is a broker-issued contract whose terms can vary.

Contract size, margin and position control

Futures come in whole contracts with a set value for each price move. That makes the math clear, but it also means your smallest position is set by the contract you choose.

An E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ) futures contract is valued at $20 per index point, while a micro contract is valued at $2 per point. If the index moves 10 points, one E-mini contract changes by $200 before fees; one micro contract changes by $20.

That isn’t the amount you pay to open the trade. Futures use margin, which is money set aside to support a leveraged position. Your broker may require more margin than the exchange minimum, and requirements can change. A small margin deposit doesn’t make the full exposure small.

CFDs often let traders choose a more flexible position size. The exact unit or value per point depends on the broker’s product specification. Nasdaq 100 CFD terms can differ from one broker to the next, so check the precise contract and value per point before you trade.

Margin ratios also vary by broker and by jurisdiction, so don’t treat any one ratio as a standard CFD setting. The margin ratio changes the cash needed to open a position, not the amount you can lose if the market moves against you.

For futures scalping, the point value helps you translate a stop into a dollar risk before entry. Say your planned stop is 10 points away. That equals $200 on one E-mini contract, or $20 on one micro contract, before fees and slippage.

A CFD’s flexible size can help a trader set a smaller exposure, but only if the broker’s minimum size and value per point allow it. For futures, smaller exposure may mean choosing a micro contract rather than an E-mini contract. Either way, size the position from the stop and the amount you can afford to lose, not from the margin figure alone.

Trading costs: commissions, spreads and financing

There isn’t one universal winner on cost. The total depends on the instrument, broker, trade size, and how long you hold the position. A low headline fee can still hide costs elsewhere.

For futures, costs may include a broker commission, exchange and clearing fees, and the bid-ask spread. You can usually review these as separate charges. For an active trader who opens and closes many positions, add up the full cost per round trip rather than comparing only the commission.

For CFDs, the spread is often the main cost. Some providers also charge a commission. A position held past the daily rollover may carry overnight financing, which can build up over several days. A short intraday trade may avoid that charge, but the spread still affects the entry and exit price.

Here’s a simple way to compare two quotes. First, note the spread when you plan to trade. Then add any commission on both sides of the trade. If you expect to hold overnight, include the financing charge as well. Compare the same position size and holding period, or the result won’t tell you much.

Costs can also change with market conditions. A wider spread means your position starts further from break-even. Thin trading hours or sudden volatility can affect fills, so a cost estimate based on a calm moment may not match what you pay during a fast move.

For a futures scalper, repeated commissions and exchange fees matter because they apply with each trade. For a CFD trader who holds positions overnight, financing can take a larger share of the total. Neither cost model is automatically cheaper. Match the calculation to your own trade frequency and holding time.

Pro Tip: Keep a cost note for one sample trade. Record spread, commissions, fees, and any overnight charge, then compare it with the same size and holding time in the other instrument.

Trading hours, expiration and holding positions

Many index futures trade close to 24 hours a day, five days a week, with a short daily break. Trading through the night can help you react to news outside regular US stock-market hours, but activity and liquidity can vary by session.

CFD hours depend on the broker. Some index CFDs follow related futures hours, but providers set their own schedules. Their quoted hours may start or stop at different times. Check the symbol’s trading hours on the platform rather than assuming the cash index schedule applies.

Futures have set expiration dates. If you want to keep the same exposure beyond expiration, you close the contract and open a later one. That’s called rolling. The price of the next contract can differ from the one you’re leaving, so the roll can affect your position’s value.

Most standard CFDs have no fixed expiration, so you may keep a position open while your account meets the broker’s margin rules. But a CFD based on a futures contract can have an expiry or a broker-managed rollover. Read the product terms. “No expiry” isn’t a safe assumption for every CFD.

For a short NQ trade around the US open, both session timing and the contract’s expiry month matter. A trader focused on the open may choose not to hold through rollover. Someone holding longer needs a plan for the contract change, including the price gap between contract months.

Set an alert for the expiry date well before it arrives. It gives you time to check liquidity in the next contract and decide whether to close or roll, rather than making that choice at the last minute.

Risk, transparency and US retail access

Both instruments carry market risk. Trading on margin can magnify gains, but it also magnifies losses. A stop order can help define a planned exit, yet a fast market can fill it at a worse price than you expected. Review the risk disclosure and terms before trading.

Futures have standardized contract terms and exchange-based pricing. That gives traders a common reference for the contract’s size and the prices being traded. The clearing structure reduces the risk tied to a trade’s other side, but it doesn’t remove the risk that your broker fails or that your position loses money.

With a CFD, you rely on the broker for the contract terms and quote. The broker is the counterparty. Prices and execution terms can differ across providers, so review the agreement and check how the firm handles client funds and open positions. A familiar chart symbol alone doesn’t tell you those details.

US access is a central part of this comparison. CFDs are generally not offered to US retail traders through US-regulated firms. Some offshore firms may target US residents, but that doesn’t make the arrangement a sound or compliant choice. Check the rules that apply to you before opening an account.

Futures are available to US retail traders through the US futures market. That availability doesn’t make them low risk. You still need to understand the contract’s value per point, margin rules, and the possibility of losing more than the amount initially set aside.

If you trade through a prop firm, its own rules add another layer. It may set limits on daily losses, contract size, or outside signals. Check those terms before following a live call. For US readers comparing futures and CFDs, the usable choice is often shaped by availability first. Then weigh exchange transparency against the broker-set terms you’d face with a CFD in a market where that product is allowed.

Which is better for you: futures or CFDs?

For a US retail trader, futures are usually the usable choice between these two instruments. CFDs may offer more flexible sizing in markets where they’re available, but US retail access is generally restricted. Don’t pick a product based on an overseas broker’s margin terms or a chart that looks like NQ.

Futures may fit you better if you want exchange-listed terms and a published contract multiplier. They also fit traders who are willing to manage fixed contract sizes and expiration dates. For Nasdaq exposure, MNQ gives a smaller per-point value than NQ, though both can move quickly.

A CFD may fit a trader in a jurisdiction where retail CFDs are permitted and who values flexible position sizing or a contract with no fixed expiry. That flexibility comes with broker-specific pricing and terms. Compare the product sheet, spread, financing, and execution rules before making a choice.

For both products, use the same test: calculate how much a planned stop costs in dollars, then include fees and possible slippage. If the trade’s risk is too large for your account or loss limit, reduce the size or skip it. A smaller margin requirement doesn’t change that calculation.

FAQ

Are futures better than CFDs?

Futures may be the better fit for US retail traders because CFDs are generally unavailable through US-regulated firms. Futures have exchange-set contract terms, while CFDs use broker-set terms. The right choice also depends on your desired position size, holding time, and risk plan. Futures still carry substantial risk, including losses that can exceed the money initially set aside.

Can US residents trade CFDs?

US retail traders generally can’t access CFDs through US-regulated firms. Some offshore providers may accept or market to US residents, but you should check the rules that apply to your location before taking action. Don’t assume that an online account offer is permitted or that the same protections apply as they would with a US futures account.

What is the difference between NQ and MNQ?

E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ) futures are valued at $20 per index point. Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 (MNQ) futures are valued at $2 per point. A 10-point move is $200 per NQ or $20 per MNQ, before fees and slippage. The smaller value per point can help you set a lower dollar exposure, but it doesn’t remove market risk.

Do CFDs have lower trading costs than futures?

Not in every case. Futures can involve commissions, exchange and clearing fees, and the spread. CFDs often charge through the spread and may add a commission or overnight financing. Your total cost depends on trade size, broker terms, and how long you hold the position. Compare the full round-trip cost for your own trading schedule.

Can I hold futures overnight?

Yes, you can hold a futures position overnight, but you still need enough margin and a plan for price changes while you’re away. Futures contracts also expire, so a longer hold may require you to roll into a later contract. Check your broker’s margin rules and the contract calendar before keeping a position open.
