NQ vs MNQ: Contract Size, Risk and Fit

By Samy Meguireche · November 2026

NQ and MNQ track the same Nasdaq-100 market, but each tick on NQ is worth ten times as much. In this NQ vs MNQ comparison, the contract math comes first, then liquidity and risk, so you can choose a size that fits your plan.

NQ vs MNQ Contract Size: Same Index, Different Exposure

NQ and MNQ are futures contracts. They track the same index, so their charts usually show very similar price moves. The difference is the contract multiplier: one NQ has the same tick value as ten MNQ contracts.

Both contracts move in minimum increments of 0.25 index points, called a tick. But the dollar value of that tick changes your exposure. A 100-point move equals $2,000 for one NQ contract and $200 for one MNQ contract, before fees. The market move is the same; the financial result is not.

That smaller step size is useful when your planned risk sits between one MNQ and one NQ. You can use several micros to approach your desired exposure instead of jumping straight to the larger contract. For a more precise calculation, you can size an NQ position using your stop distance and risk limit.

Key takeaway: One NQ and ten MNQ have the same tick value, before fees. One MNQ carries one-tenth the exposure of one NQ.

Tick Values and Stop Risk: What a 75-Tick Move Costs

A stop only makes sense when you know what it costs if price reaches it. For one contract, multiply the number of ticks between entry and stop by the dollar value per tick. That gives you price risk before commissions and fees.

Take a 75-tick stop. On one MNQ, the calculation is 75 × $0.50, or $37.50. On one NQ, it is 75 × $5, or $375. The stop is the same distance on the chart, but the NQ loss is ten times larger if price hits it.

Several MNQ contracts let you make smaller changes to that exposure. For example, four MNQ contracts with the same 75-tick stop would put $150 at risk before fees. Ten MNQ contracts would match one NQ’s $375 price risk for that stop distance.

Those amounts are not a suggested risk limit. Your account size, trading plan and ability to accept a loss all matter. A smaller contract does not make Nasdaq price action calmer. It only reduces the dollar effect of each tick. The market can still move quickly, and a stop can be filled at a different price during fast conditions.

Before placing an order, work out the loss at your stop. Then check that the amount fits your plan without changing the stop just to make a larger contract feel affordable.

Liquidity, Slippage and Order Execution

The larger contract generally has more trading activity than the smaller contract, while the smaller contract’s size makes it easier to adjust exposure in small steps. Trading activity can change with the session and market conditions, so don’t assume either contract will always have the same spread or fill quality. Volume measures contracts traded over a period and can help show market activity.

For a market order, slippage means your fill price differs from the price you expected. A wider bid-ask spread can also raise the cost of entering or leaving a trade. These effects matter most when your stop is tight or you’re trading during a fast move. Check the live spread and order-book conditions on the contract you plan to trade.

Some traders chart the larger contract but place orders in the smaller contract. The idea is to use the larger contract’s chart to read price levels, then use the smaller contract to set exposure more precisely. That workflow can help with position sizing, but it doesn’t guarantee matching fills: small differences in price prints or wicks can occur between contracts.

Before you send an order, check that the symbol in your chart matches the symbol in the order-entry window. A correct chart with the wrong contract selected can still lead to an order with a different dollar risk than you planned.

Pro Tip: If you chart the larger contract and execute the smaller contract, verify the order symbol and calculate the stop risk from the smaller contract’s tick value before submitting.

Which Is Better for Your Account, Experience and Risk Tolerance?

For many new futures traders, MNQ is the more manageable place to start. A smaller dollar swing can make it easier to stick to a pre-set stop while you learn how your strategy behaves. It does not remove trading risk or make a setup more likely to work.

NQ may fit a trader whose risk plan supports its larger tick value and who wants to trade fewer contracts. But if a 75-tick stop would risk $375 on one contract, that loss needs to be acceptable before entry. If it isn’t, reducing the stop distance just to use NQ can change the trade’s plan rather than solve the sizing problem.

MNQ also gives you more control when you want to scale out of a position. With several micros, you can close part of the trade while keeping the rest open. One NQ contract can’t be split into smaller contract units. Still, adding more micros can bring your total risk close to NQ’s, so count the full position rather than focusing on the small value of one contract.

Use current contract details alongside your broker’s margin and fee terms before trading. Margin is the amount your broker requires to hold a position, not the maximum you can lose.

NQ vs MNQ FAQ

Do NQ and MNQ move the same?

They track the same Nasdaq-100 market, so their price charts usually move very closely together. Their dollar exposure differs because MNQ is one-tenth the size of NQ. Small differences in price prints or wicks can occur, so don’t assume that a level or fill will match perfectly across both contracts.

How much is one NQ tick worth?

One NQ tick is worth $5, and one index point is worth $20. NQ’s minimum price increment is 0.25 points, which equals one tick. Multiply the number of ticks between your entry and stop by $5 to estimate one-contract price risk before commissions and fees.

How much is one MNQ tick worth?

One MNQ tick is worth $0.50, and one index point is worth $2. A 0.25-point move equals one tick. For one MNQ contract, a 75-tick stop equals $37.50 in price risk before commissions and fees. More contracts increase the total risk in proportion to your position size.

Is MNQ better for beginners than NQ?

MNQ is often a more manageable starting point because each tick has a smaller dollar value. That can help you keep a trade within your planned risk while learning to follow a strategy. It doesn’t make the market less volatile or protect you from losses. Pick the contract by calculating your stop risk first.

Can I chart NQ and trade MNQ?

Yes, some traders use the NQ chart to read price levels and place trades in MNQ for finer position sizing. Check that the order window is set to MNQ, then calculate risk using $0.50 per tick per contract. Fills can differ between the contracts, so monitor the contract you actually trade.

Conclusion

Choose MNQ if NQ’s dollar swings exceed the risk you’ve set; consider NQ only when its larger exposure fits your plan.
