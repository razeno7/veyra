How to Trade NQ Futures: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Samy Meguireche · November 2026

Trading NQ moves fast, so a clear plan matters before you place an order. If you’re learning how to trade NQ futures, start with the contract, set a loss limit, and choose one setup to test instead of chasing every move.

Step 1: Learn the NQ Contract and Trading Session

Your first goal is to know what one contract means in dollars and when you plan to trade it. NQ is a futures contract. Before trading, learn how its price moves and what each point means in dollars.

The contract size shown for NQ is 20. In usable terms, a one-point move represents $20 per NQ contract before fees and slippage. A 10-point move against one contract would mean a $200 loss before those costs. That move can happen quickly, so don’t treat a small chart distance as a small dollar risk.

Futures have expiration dates. Check which contract month your platform is showing, especially as the market approaches a roll to a later contract.

A smaller-sized futures contract can provide lower dollar exposure. It doesn’t make a trade safe by itself. Your stop distance still determines how much you can lose.

Pick a trading window

Choose your session before you open the chart. The US stock market opens at 9:30 a.m. Eastern Time. NQ can move sharply around that time, and the first burst of activity can make a market order fill at a different price than you expected.

You can also mark the high and low formed from 3:00 a.m. ET up to the US open. Some traders watch these pre-market levels for a break, a retest, or a failed break that returns inside the range. Treat each as a possible setup, not a signal to enter automatically.

Another way to frame the open is to mark the first 15-minute range, from 9:30 to 9:45 a.m. ET, then watch a five-minute chart for a close outside it. A close beyond the range can show a push, but it can also fail. Decide in advance what confirmation you need and where your trade idea is wrong.

Milestone: You should be able to say which contract you’re trading, what each point means in dollars, and what session your plan covers.

Step 2: Choose a Broker, Platform, and Contract Size

Your goal is to make sure the account can support your plan before you place a live order. A futures broker sets its own margin rules. Margin is the amount of money it requires to hold a position, and it isn’t the same as the most you can lose. Losses can exceed the amount you set aside for a trade.

Check the broker’s current initial and maintenance margin for NQ and MNQ. Ask whether its intraday margin differs from its overnight requirement. Also read its liquidation rules. A broker may close positions when account equity falls below its threshold, and that can happen before your planned stop is reached.

On the platform, find the exact contract month and confirm the order ticket is set to futures. Learn how to place a market order, a limit order, and a stop order in simulation first. Check what the platform displays for quantity, order type, and average entry before you send an order.

For example, imagine a 10-point stop and a planned $100 loss. At $20 per point, one NQ contract would risk $200 before costs, so it exceeds that example limit. MNQ represents $2 per point. Five MNQ contracts would risk $100 before costs at the same stop distance. That’s arithmetic, not a recommended position size.

Check the math again if you change the stop or add contracts. A position-size calculator can help, but it won’t know whether your stop is placed at a useful chart level. You still have to choose the risk limit and the stop location.

Step 3: Set Your Risk Limits Before the Session

Your goal is to decide how much you can lose before the market starts moving. Do this while you’re calm. A stop and a daily limit are only useful if you follow them after an uncomfortable trade.

Start with a maximum loss per trade. Then choose a daily loss limit that tells you when to stop for the day. The right number depends on your account and your financial situation. Don’t borrow a limit from another trader or raise it because you’re trying to win back a loss.

Size the trade from the stop

First, mark where the trade idea would be invalid. That price is your planned stop. Measure the distance between the entry and stop in points. Multiply that distance by the dollar value per point, then multiply by the number of contracts.

For NQ, use $20 per point per contract in the calculation. For MNQ, use $2 per point per contract. Include room for fees and slippage, since your fill may not match the price you planned. If the result is too large, reduce the number of contracts, consider MNQ, or skip the trade.

Don’t move a stop farther away just to avoid taking a loss. If the chart requires a wider stop than your risk limit allows, the setup may not fit your account. That’s a reason to pass, not a reason to ignore the limit.

Set a stop point for the day

Decide how many losing trades you can take before you pause. Set a daily loss cap in your platform if it has that function, but keep your own written rule too. After you hit the limit, stop trading. Don’t switch to a faster chart or increase size to make the money back.

Build a short pre-session check. Confirm the contract month, your risk per trade, and the loss point that ends your session. Also check whether a scheduled market event could make your planned stop hard to execute. If the conditions don’t fit your plan, staying flat is a valid decision.

Pro Tip: Write the dollar risk beside the chart stop before you enter. If you can’t state the planned loss in one sentence, wait.

Milestone: Before the session, you should have a stop level, a maximum trade risk, and a firm point at which you’ll stop trading for the day.

Step 4: Build a Repeatable NQ Trade Setup

Your goal is to define one setup tightly enough that you can recognize it without making up new rules mid-trade. A setup needs a market context, an entry trigger, a stop point, and an exit plan. More indicators won’t fix a vague plan.

Start with a five-minute chart if you want to study the opening range or pre-market levels. Mark the high and low from 3:00 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. ET, then note the first 15-minute range after the open. You can test whether a break-and-retest fits your style: price closes past a marked level, pulls back, and then shows a response in the direction of the break.

Write down what counts as a valid close and what would make you pass. For instance, you might require a five-minute candle to close beyond the level, then wait for price to test it from the other side. If price snaps back into the range, that may invalidate the breakout idea. It doesn’t prove a reversal is coming.

Keep pullbacks simple

A pullback setup starts with a clear move in one direction. Rather than enter after a large candle, wait for price to retrace toward a level you marked. Some traders use a moving average, such as a 21-period exponential moving average, as one reference. It is not a stand-alone entry signal.

You can test a two-leg pullback rule: after an established trend, wait for two attempts to move against it. Then require a clear signal bar in the trend direction before entering. Define the signal bar and the entry trigger before you test. If you change those definitions from trade to trade, your journal won’t tell you whether the setup works for you.

Know the difference between a break and a sweep

A breakout setup looks for price to close beyond a level and hold or retest it. A liquidity sweep describes price moving briefly beyond a prior high or low, then returning across that level. Traders may watch for that return as evidence that the breakout failed. It can still keep moving against you, so the stop belongs in the plan.

Volume can add context, but don’t treat a volume increase as proof that a breakout will hold. In your notes, record what you saw at the level, what triggered the entry, and what would have invalidated the idea. Keep the rules narrow enough to test on replay or in simulation.

Milestone: You should be able to describe the setup in a few clear rules and identify conditions that tell you to pass.

Step 5: Place, Manage, and Exit the Trade

Your goal is to place the order you planned, not to improvise as each price tick changes. Before entry, check the symbol, contract month, quantity, order type, and direction. A quick mistake in the order ticket can turn a planned trade into a different position.

Choose the order that matches your plan

A market order aims to enter promptly, but the fill price can differ from the quote you saw. A limit order sets the worst price you’ll accept, but it may not fill. A stop order can trigger an entry after price reaches a set level, though the final fill may differ during a fast move.

Pick the order type before the setup appears. If your plan calls for a candle close beyond a level, wait for the close. Don’t enter early because price is close. If the move leaves without you, let it go rather than chasing it with a wider stop.

Place the stop and target

Set the protective stop as soon as your entry is active, unless your platform lets you submit the entry and attached exits together. Check that the stop quantity matches the position. A stop order can help limit risk, but it can’t guarantee a fill at the exact stop price.

Set a target based on the plan you tested. It could be a marked level or a fixed distance, but choose it before entry. If you plan to scale out or move the stop, write down the rule for doing so. Avoid changing a target just because the trade is briefly in profit.

For a simple R example, the distance from entry to stop is 1R. If the stop is 10 points away and a target is 20 points away, the target is 2R before fees and slippage. A stopped-out signal is −1R when it loses the planned risk. Your own result can differ because of execution, size, and your decisions.

Monitor the trade without moving your stop farther away. If the stop is hit, accept the result and follow your daily limit. If the target is reached, exit according to the plan. If the trade stalls, use only the time-based exit rule you set before entry.

Prop-firm traders need one extra check before following any outside signal: read the firm’s rules. Some firms restrict third-party signals or copy trading. A valid setup on a chart doesn’t override your account agreement.

Milestone: The live position should match your planned contract, stop, target, and maximum loss. If one of those changes, know the rule that permits it.

Step 6: Review Results and Refine Your Process

Your goal is to find out whether you followed your rules, not to judge your skill by one winning or losing trade. Review each trade after the session, once the pressure has passed. Keep the plan and the result separate.

Record the date, contract, time, direction, entry, stop, target, and result in R. Add a chart image if your platform allows it. Note whether you followed the setup, whether you moved the stop, and what market condition you saw at entry. Keep the note factual. “I entered before the close” is more useful than “I was bad today.”

Review a group of trades before changing your method. Ask whether losses came from the setup or from breaking your own rules. Check whether results differ between breakout trades and pullbacks. Also compare the open with other times you trade. This helps you avoid changing a rule because of one unusual session.

Keep a short weekly review. Look for repeated errors, such as entries taken before confirmation or size that exceeds the planned risk. Choose one behavior to work on next week. If you change several rules at once, you won’t know which change affected the results.

FAQ

How much money do I need to trade NQ futures?

There isn’t one account amount that fits every trader. Your broker sets margin requirements, and your risk depends on the number of contracts and the distance to your stop. Check current margin with your broker, then calculate the planned loss before entry. Margin is not your maximum loss, and a small account can face fast losses.

Is MNQ better for beginners than NQ?

MNQ has one-tenth the contract size of NQ, so each point carries less dollar exposure. That can make it easier to match a small risk limit, but it doesn’t remove the risk of loss. You still need a planned stop and a position size that fits your limit. Practice order entry before risking live funds.

What time is best to trade NQ?

There’s no single best time for every trader or setup. Many plans focus on the US open at 9:30 a.m. ET, when the stock market opens and activity can shift quickly. Choose one trading window, test it, and record your results. Don’t assume a time is right for you just because other traders focus on it.

Can I trade NQ with a prop firm account?

You may be able to, but each prop firm sets its own rules. Check whether it allows third-party trading signals or copy trading before you follow a signal on a challenge or funded account. You’re responsible for following the firm’s limits. Signals and live sessions can’t guarantee that you’ll pass or keep a funded account.
